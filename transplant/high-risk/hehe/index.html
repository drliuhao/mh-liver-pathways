Observation and the need for transplantation

Proposed position for discussion — not an activated protocol

  • In stable disease, propose six months of largely stable biology before final a final decision to proceed with transplantation, with comparable staging and approximately eight-week reassessment. Reliable earlier imaging can count; document the actual diagnosis, staging, registration and observation dates rather than restart a clock at referral.
  • Do not delay threatening liver failure, significant progressive symptoms or rapid progression solely to complete six months. Obtain urgent multidisciplinary review of LT versus non-LT treatment; urgency does not itself establish oncologic suitability.
  • Six months is an expert-guidance-informed local default, not a national requirement or validated cutoff. Continued stability does not mandate LT: reassess resection, symptom burden, function and benefit over continued observation.
Tests and information to review

Expert EHE pathology; complete liver/systemic staging; symptoms and liver function; interval growth and vascular/node assessment; resection review.

Allocation and donor route

This is a proposed clinical timing choice, not an added NLRB exception requirement. Standard HEHE protocol content is not rewritten in this edition.

Evidence, limitations and supporting papers

Evidence summary

Observation can expose indolent biology and avoid unnecessary graft use. The 120-day signal is prognostic association among recipients, not a tested intervention. Good US outcomes with a shorter median wait and poor outcomes in a small high-burden cohort both argue for clinical context rather than a stopwatch. DGVS explicitly favors six months of largely stable disease. This expert-guidance counterpoint is more conservative than the earlier four-month local proposal; neither interval has causal comparative validation.

Evidence against expansion

There is no randomized immediate-versus-delayed LT comparison. Time accrued before referral, urgent liver failure, selection to survive waiting, lead time and different OS clocks can generate apparent benefits of observation.

Certainty

Low certainty for selected post-LT survival; very low certainty that a fixed observation interval improves outcomes. No validated universal 120-day eligibility cliff. Lai measured waiting time from waiting-list registration, not completed staging, diagnosis or referral; our proposed staging-based clock is an explicit adaptation.

Basis for proposed thresholds

  • Six months: international expert-guidance default, not US policy or a proven biological cliff. Reliable pre-referral stability may contribute.
  • 120 days: Lai registry exposure measured from waiting-list registration to LT; it is not the same as diagnosis-to-treatment or completed-staging observation and does not causally validate four months.
  • Approximately eight-week reassessment and exceptions: local workflow/safety judgment; adjust to disease trajectory.

Lai 2017

Design and population. Retrospective ELTR/ELITA; 149 adult LT recipients, 1984–2014; median post-LT follow-up 7.6 years.

Selection. Transplanted patients only; no randomized observation policy.

Results. Post-LT 5-year OS 79.5%; 37/149 recurred. Waiting ≤120 days associated with recurrence: Table 5 adjusted HR 2.5 (95% CI 1.2–5.3); 5-year DFS 73.3% versus 86.7% with longer waiting.

Limits. Confounding by urgency and survivorship; association does not show that waiting causes benefit. Earlier ELTR series overlap. Abstract HR differs slightly from Table 5.

Brahmbhatt 2020

Design and population. UNOS 2002–2018: 131 adults listed; 88 transplanted.

Selection. Median MELD at LT 7; median wait 78.5 days (IQR 29.5–237.5); 120/131 sought exceptions.

Results. Post-LT 1/3/5-year OS 88.6%/78.9%/77.2%. Early hepatic-artery-thrombosis graft failure 4.6% versus 0.5% in non-HEHE comparators.

Limits. Abstract-only extraction. Favorable survival despite median wait <120 days argues against a proven mandatory four-month threshold; not a randomized timing comparison. Older UNOS reports overlap.

Thomas 2014

Design and population. Single-center retrospective cohort, n=50; initial surgery 7, systemic/embolization 18, observation 25.

Selection. Histologically confirmed EHE; 24/50 metastatic at diagnosis; treatment selected clinically.

Results. Five-year OS from diagnosis: surgery 83.3%, systemic/embolization 71.3%, observation 72.4% (P=.9). Localized versus metastatic: 69% versus 78.3% (P=.7).

Limits. Small, nonrandom groups; no metastatic patients in initial surgery group. Supports observing selected indolent disease, not equivalence of therapies or a 120-day cutoff.

Liu 2024

Design and population. Single-team retrospective cohort 2014–2024: 228 evaluable; LT 8, resection/ablation 49, nonsurgical 171.

Selection. Half the eight LT patients had a lesion >10 cm, versus 10% of non-LT patients.

Results. Overall 5-year OS 84.9%; LT 1/3-year OS 62.5%/25%; non-LT 5-year OS 87.8%. Survival time zero is not clearly specified in the extracted Methods; do not relabel as standard post-LT estimates.

Limits. Major confounding by indication, eight recipients and loss-to-follow-up exclusion. Poor LT results cannot prove LT causes harm; they challenge assuming all HEHE has excellent transplant outcomes.

Stacchiotti 2021

Design and population. International expert consensus; evidence predominantly retrospective and very-low certainty.

Selection. Distinguishes asymptomatic/stable systemic EHE from unresectable liver-confined disease.

Results. Supports initial active surveillance for suitable stable disease and consideration of LT for selected unresectable liver-only EHE; advises against LT with EHD (V,A).

Limits. Expert recommendation conflicts with ACG key concepts and NLRB guidance. Neither side has randomized EHD transplant-benefit evidence.

DGVS S2k LT guidance

Design and population. German S2k consensus guideline; HEHE recommendations dated 2023, publication 2024.

Selection. Recommendation 3.86 addresses stable disease before LT; 3.85 concerns eligibility for evaluation.

Results. Recommends at least six months of largely stable disease before LT. Macrovascular invasion or hilar nodes are not absolute barriers to evaluation, which is not equivalent to recommending transplantation.

Limits. Expert consensus, not a randomized timing comparison or US allocation rule. It supplies a competing six-month option, not proof of superiority to four months.

Extrahepatic disease and vascular invasion

Proposed position for discussion — not an activated protocol

  • Proposed opening boundary: unresectable liver-only EHE with no macrovascular invasion. Extrahepatic disease is a separate unactivated stratum requiring case-specific discussion, not a national absolute prohibition.
  • Resolve suspicious nodes/extrahepatic lesions before proceeding. Do not use an explant-derived score as if it were a validated pretransplant eligibility test.
  • Record the guideline disagreement explicitly. A future EHD proposal must specify site, control duration, symptoms, comparator and independent review; “stable EHD” alone is insufficient.
Tests and information to review

Complete staging; expert pathology; vascular assessment; targeted evaluation of suspicious extrahepatic sites; technical resectability and alternative-treatment review.

Allocation and donor route

Clinical selection and exception-review eligibility answer different questions. The proposed liver-only choice is local, not a rewrite of national guidance.

Evidence, limitations and supporting papers

Evidence summary

The disagreement is genuine: ACG/NLRB are permissive about selected EHD, whereas the disease-specific consensus is restrictive. The best registry suggests macrovascular invasion and hilar nodes are adverse; selected EHD was not independently adverse, but the study was too small to establish equivalence. Histology-derived risk scoring cannot be substituted for preoperative staging.

Evidence against expansion

A blanket statement that any EHD absolutely forbids LT is inaccurate. Conversely, favorable survival in selected EHD recipients does not establish benefit over observation or systemic options for all sites/burdens.

Certainty

Low certainty for prognostic association; very low for causal EHD benefit or pre-LT score-driven selection.

Basis for proposed thresholds

  • No macrovascular invasion: aligns with current favorable NLRB consideration and adverse ELTR prognosis.
  • Liver-only opening cohort: conservative local choice despite permissive ACG/NLRB wording; cannot be called universally guideline-mandated.
  • EHE-LT score cutoffs: post-LT risk stratification, not a validated pre-LT exclusion test.

Lai 2017

Design and population. Same 149-recipient ELTR series; 40 had pre-LT extrahepatic disease.

Selection. EHD comparison restricted to 23 matched pairs; score uses explant/pathological variables.

Results. Matched 5-year DFS: EHD 73.5% versus 81.6% without, P=.54. macrovascular invasion adjusted recurrence HR 4.9 (2.4–9.9); hilar nodes HR 2.2 (1.1–4.5). Score groups 0–2/3–5/6–10: 5-year DFS 93.9%/76.9%/38.5%.

Limits. A nonsignificant small comparison is not equivalence. Score was not validated as a pre-LT eligibility test; grade/stage can be missed before explant.

ACG 2024

Design and population. ACG 2024 focal liver lesion guideline, key concepts 34–36.

Selection. Whole-body CT/MRI staging; recognize variable, sometimes low PET avidity.

Results. Key concept 35 supports resection if possible and LT when unresectable, including selected EHD. This is a clinical position, not an allocation entitlement.

Limits. These statements are key concepts, not GRADE-rated recommendations; optimistic comparative language rests on selected series.

NLRB oncology guidance

Design and population. Official December 2025 transplant-oncology exception guidance, current posting checked 24 October 2026.

Selection. HEHE diagnosis, unresectability and absence of macrovascular invasion are separately considered.

Results. EHD is not an absolute contraindication in this guidance. No mandatory four-month observation rule is specified.

Limits. Nonbinding allocation-review guidance; does not demonstrate individual clinical benefit or override donor/clinical judgment.
